03 / WHAT STANDS OUT

Small differences early. Much larger differences later.

Across all five pathways, the difference between the highest and lowest decadal model means is only 0.11°C in 2021–2030. By 2091–2100, it grows to 3.81°C. Moving the decade slider makes the widening gap visible.

The low-emissions SSP1-1.9 pathway peaks around mid-century in this dataset and then declines slightly, while the higher-emissions scenarios continue to rise. Because each pathway has a different set of contributing models, these differences should be read as a descriptive comparison—not a controlled estimate of the effect of changing emissions.

04 / METHODS & DESIGN

About this visualization

Question and motivation

Climate projections are often summarized with a single end-of-century number. Our question is how the distance between pathways changes over time. Comparing decadal trajectories makes it possible to see both early similarity and eventual divergence without implying knowledge of the exact weather in any individual year.

Visual encodings

We use horizontal position for time and vertical position for a quantitative temperature anomaly, which supports accurate comparisons of magnitude. Color identifies scenario, not temperature severity. All series share the same axes; direct labels at the right edge reduce the need to match colors to a separate legend. Straight line segments connect decadal means and do not represent underlying annual observations.

Why these interactions?

Checkboxes remove clutter when focusing on particular pathways. The warming/difference switch answers a second question without introducing a different dataset: how much warmer is each pathway than SSP1-1.9 in the same decade? Hover inspection and the decade slider connect a long-term trend to a specific decade. The linked ranking and spread charts make differences that are small or hard to judge on the main chart much easier to compare.

We considered a geographic map and stacked area chart. A map would imply geographic detail that our globally averaged data does not contain; stacked areas would also obscure independent temperature levels. A common-scale line chart paired with a linked comparison is a closer match for this research question.

Dataset and transformations

We use CMIP6 global mean surface-air temperature (variable tas) as compiled in Mathias Hauser's CMIP TEMPERATURES repository. The source computes global annual mean anomalies relative to 1850–1900 for each model, then raw multi-model means using one simulation per model. We selected the eight non-overlapping ten-year periods from 2021–2030 through 2091–2100 and retained five SSP scenario columns. The supplied CSV contains these 40 published, two-decimal values. No scenarios or outliers were removed.

The interface filters scenarios and computes two additional measures: difference from SSP1-1.9 within each decade, and spread = maximum minus minimum of the visible scenario means. These are simple arithmetic comparisons, not new climate-model simulations.

Limits and interpretation

These are raw, unassessed model averages, not observations or the IPCC's assessed warming ranges. Model counts differ by scenario (SSP1-1.9: 14; SSP1-2.6: 41; SSP2-4.5: 42; SSP3-7.0: 35; SSP5-8.5: 44). An ensemble of opportunity, rather than a common matched set of models, contributes to the means. The chart also omits uncertainty ranges. The dashed 2°C line is a reading aid; crossing it in these decadal means does not determine an official warming-level year.
